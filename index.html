Australia’s visitor economy is rebuilding — but not evenly

Tourism is one of Australia's largest and most influential industries, connecting domestic travel, international aviation, hospitality, education, and regional economies into a single visitor network. Every year, millions of Australians travel interstate while international visitors arrive from major global markets across Asia, Europe, and North America. Together, these journeys generate billions in visitor spending and support hundreds of thousands of jobs nationwide. Yet the industry was transformed by the COVID-19 pandemic, which triggered the sharpest collapse in modern tourism history. While travel demand has returned strongly since borders reopened, the recovery has been uneven across states, cities, and international markets. Some destinations have surpassed pre-pandemic levels, while others continue to rely on the slow return of high-value overseas visitors.

The national picture — growth, collapse, and recovery

Before examining where visitors travel and how they spend, it is important to understand the scale of Australia's tourism industry. Domestic travel drives the majority of visitor activity, while international tourism contributes disproportionately to total spending. The charts below show how both sectors expanded before collapsing during COVID-19 and gradually recovering after borders reopened.

Section 1 — the big picture

Tourism rebounded — but the recovery remains uneven

Australia's tourism industry operates at two very different scales. Domestic tourism dominates in raw visitor numbers and overnight stays, reflecting the size of Australia's internal travel market and the importance of interstate leisure and visiting friends and relatives (VFR) travel. International tourism, however, generates significantly higher spending per visitor and plays a critical role in supporting aviation, hospitality, retail, and education sectors. The pandemic disrupted both markets simultaneously, causing an unprecedented collapse in international arrivals and a sharp decline in domestic movement. Although tourism activity has rebounded strongly since 2022, the pace of recovery differs considerably between states and international markets.

The chart below compares domestic and international visitor nights across each Australian state and territory. Each dot represents approximately one million visitor nights, making it easy to see both the scale and the geographic imbalance of Australia's visitor economy at a glance.

The sheer volume gap between NSW and the rest of Australia is striking. While every state contributes to the national visitor economy, the eastern seaboard — and Sydney in particular — accounts for a disproportionate share of both domestic and international visitor activity.

Key insight

New South Wales dominates Australia's tourism industry across both domestic and international markets. Sydney's role as the country's largest international gateway allows NSW to attract more visitor nights than any other state, reinforcing the concentration of tourism activity along Australia's eastern seaboard.

To understand how this picture has changed over time, the two charts below trace the long-run trajectory of international arrivals and the total volume of visitor nights across both markets. The COVID-19 collapse — and the subsequent recovery — is the defining feature of both series.

International arrivals fell to near zero in 2020 and remained suppressed through 2021, the longest continuous border closure in Australia's modern history. Domestic visitor nights also declined sharply, though the domestic market began recovering earlier as internal travel restrictions eased before international borders reopened in February 2022. By 2024, total visitor nights had largely returned to pre-pandemic levels — but the composition of the recovery differs markedly by country of origin.

Key insight

By 2024, Australia had recovered most of its pre-pandemic international visitor numbers, but the rebound remains uneven. Markets such as India and the United States have grown beyond 2019 levels, while China — previously Australia's most valuable tourism market — remains substantially below its pre-pandemic peak.

These broad national trends, however, mask significant geographic variation. The next question is not just how much tourism activity exists across Australia, but where it concentrates — and why some states and cities attract far more visitors and spending than others.

2
Tourism activity is concentrated along Australia's eastern seaboard

Although tourism contributes to every Australian state and territory, visitor activity is distributed unevenly across the country. Population density, international airports, and established tourism infrastructure heavily influence where travellers go and where spending is concentrated.

Section 2 — where in australia

Where tourism activity concentrates

Tourism activity in Australia is highly concentrated in the eastern states, particularly New South Wales, Victoria, and Queensland. These states combine large population centres with major international airports, established accommodation networks, and globally recognised attractions. While regional tourism remains economically important, the majority of visitor spending is concentrated within major metropolitan areas such as Sydney and Melbourne. This imbalance highlights how access, connectivity, and international aviation continue to shape Australia's tourism geography. States with limited international air connections — such as South Australia, Tasmania, and the Northern Territory — attract fewer overnight visitors despite offering distinct natural and cultural experiences.

The maps below show two complementary views of Australia's tourism geography: domestic overnight trip volumes by state on the left, and total visitor spending by capital city on the right. Together they reveal not just where Australians travel most, but where tourism dollars are most heavily concentrated.

The domestic trip map reflects population distribution — NSW, Victoria, and Queensland together account for the vast majority of overnight travel. But the spending map tells a more nuanced story. Sydney's bubble dwarfs every other capital city, reflecting its dual role as both a major domestic destination and the country's primary international gateway. Melbourne records comparable trip volumes to Sydney in the domestic market, yet generates considerably less total visitor spend — a gap largely explained by the higher average expenditure of international visitors concentrated in Sydney.

Key insight

Sydney generates substantially more visitor spending than any other Australian city despite recording trip volumes similar to Melbourne. This difference is driven largely by international tourism, particularly high-spending visitors arriving from long-haul markets such as China, the United States, and the United Kingdom.

The concentration of visitors in Sydney and Melbourne raises a deeper question: where are these international visitors actually coming from, and what makes certain overseas markets so much more economically valuable than others? Visitor volume and visitor spending do not always move together — understanding that gap is key to understanding Australia's international tourism strategy.

3
Australia's tourism economy depends heavily on international connections

Australia's international tourism industry relies on a relatively small group of overseas markets and major aviation corridors. Visitor volume alone does not determine economic importance — some markets contribute far greater spending per visitor than others.

Section 3 — international visitors

Who visits Australia — and who spends the most

Australia attracts visitors from across the world, but international tourism is dominated by a relatively small number of source markets concentrated within the Asia-Pacific region. New Zealand contributes the highest visitor volume due to geographic proximity and strong trans-Tasman connections, while markets such as China, India, the United States, and the United Kingdom generate disproportionately high visitor spending. International aviation networks play a critical role in sustaining these visitor flows, with major hub airports such as Singapore and Dubai acting as essential gateways between Australia and long-haul markets. The structure of these aviation connections means that any disruption to key hub routes can have an outsized effect on total international arrivals, as was demonstrated during the pandemic when the collapse of hub connectivity amplified the impact of border closures far beyond what direct-route shutdowns alone would have caused.

The world map shows the geographic distribution of visitors to Australia using a logarithmic colour scale, making variation visible across both high- and low-volume markets. Use the region dropdown to zoom into specific parts of the world.

The Asia-Pacific region dominates, with New Zealand, China, the United Kingdom, the United States, and India accounting for the largest visitor flows. Europe contributes meaningful volumes through the UK and a handful of continental markets, while the Americas and Africa remain comparatively minor sources. This geographic concentration creates both opportunity and vulnerability: strong growth in any single major market can meaningfully lift national totals, but so can a decline.

Key insight

The relationship between visitor volume and visitor spending is uneven across international markets. Although New Zealand sends the highest number of visitors to Australia, Chinese visitors contribute significantly more spending per trip, making China one of Australia's most economically valuable tourism markets despite sending fewer total visitors.

The charts below break down the top ten visitor markets by total arrivals and compare each country's trip volume against its total spending. This comparison reveals which markets punch above their weight economically and which rely on volume rather than high per-visitor expenditure.

The scatter plot is particularly revealing. Countries that sit above the trend line spend more relative to their trip volume — these are the high-value markets. China, despite its slower post-pandemic recovery, remains one of the most economically significant, generating spending far out of proportion to its current arrival numbers. India sits in a different position: growing fast in volume and beginning to show meaningful spending growth too, suggesting it may become an increasingly important market across both dimensions in the coming years.

Key insight

Australia's tourism industry depends heavily on major international aviation corridors. Routes connecting Sydney with Auckland, Singapore, and Dubai function as critical gateways for international arrivals, meaning disruptions to these hubs can significantly affect Australia's visitor economy.

Understanding where visitors come from is only half the story. What they do once they arrive — and how much they spend doing it — reveals which parts of the Australian tourism experience generate the most economic value, and which visitor types are most worth attracting. Purpose of travel, it turns out, is one of the strongest predictors of spending behaviour.

4
Purpose shapes how visitors spend and experience Australia

Not all tourists behave the same way once they arrive. Travel purpose strongly influences how long visitors stay, how much they spend, and which activities they participate in throughout their trip.

Section 4 — purpose & activities

Purpose & activities

Visitor behaviour differs substantially between domestic and international tourism markets. Domestic travel is heavily influenced by visiting friends and relatives, short leisure trips, and interstate holidays, while international tourism is more strongly associated with long-haul holidays, education, and business travel. These differences have major economic implications because some visitor types generate significantly higher spending than others. Education visitors, for example, typically stay for months rather than days, accumulating accommodation, living, and transport costs that far exceed the expenditure of a typical leisure tourist. Beyond accommodation and transport, tourism spending is closely linked to dining, entertainment, retail, and nature-based experiences, highlighting the broad economic reach of Australia's visitor economy and its connections to industries that sit well beyond the traditional tourism sector.

The charts below examine how expenditure breaks down by travel purpose for both domestic and international visitors, and what activities international visitors most commonly engage in during their stay. Together they reveal the full breadth of economic activity that tourism generates.

Holiday and leisure spending dominates both domestic and international totals, but education stands out as a disproportionately high-value category for international visitors. The activity hierarchy shows that dining, sightseeing, and shopping are near-universal behaviours among international visitors, making the hospitality and retail sectors deeply intertwined with tourism outcomes. Nature-based and cultural activities feature prominently too, underlining the role that Australia's natural landscape and heritage play in driving visitor interest.

Key insight

Education tourism generates some of the highest visitor spending in Australia despite representing a relatively small share of total arrivals. International students stay longer and spend substantially more than leisure tourists, making education one of Australia's most valuable tourism sectors on a per-visitor basis.

The bar chart below ranks the specific activities that international visitors participate in most frequently. These participation rates matter not just for understanding visitor experience, but for identifying which parts of the economy benefit most from tourism demand.

Eating out and visiting friends and relatives top the list, reinforcing that international tourism is as much a social and culinary experience as a sightseeing one. Nature tourism — visiting parks, beaches, and wildlife — also features strongly, consistent with Australia's international brand as a destination defined by its natural environment. These activity patterns have direct implications for where policy investment in tourism infrastructure generates the greatest return.

Activity patterns reveal what visitors value about Australia — its food, its nature, its cities. But the industry's ability to serve those visitors depends critically on how quickly it has recovered from the most disruptive event in its modern history. Not every market, state, or destination has bounced back at the same pace, and the gaps that remain have real consequences for jobs, investment, and long-term growth.

5
Recovery continues — but some markets remain far behind

Australia's tourism industry has recovered strongly since borders reopened, but the rebound has not occurred evenly across all visitor markets. Some countries have already surpassed pre-pandemic levels, while others remain well below their former peak.

Section 5 — timing & recovery

The uneven road back from COVID

The COVID-19 pandemic reshaped Australia's tourism industry more dramatically than any previous disruption in modern history. International arrivals collapsed almost entirely during border closures, while domestic tourism fluctuated alongside lockdowns and travel restrictions. Although visitor activity has recovered rapidly since 2022, the rebound differs significantly between countries and Australian states. Markets such as India and the United States have returned strongly and in some cases surpassed their 2019 visitor numbers, while China continues to recover more slowly due to a combination of changed travel preferences, reduced direct aviation capacity, and bilateral diplomatic factors. These differences matter because the pace of international recovery will determine which destinations, airlines, and tourism sectors benefit most over the coming decade. A recovery led by high-volume, lower-spend markets looks very different from one driven by the return of China's high-value visitors.

The heatmap on the left shows monthly arrival patterns across 2019 and 2022–2024, making it possible to see both the seasonality of Australian tourism and how quickly each year has approached the pre-pandemic baseline. The slope chart on the right directly compares each country's 2019 arrival volume to its 2024 figure, with green lines indicating markets that have exceeded their pre-pandemic peak and red lines those still falling short.

The seasonality heatmap shows that Australia's peak arrival months have remained consistent through the recovery — summer and the Christmas holiday period continue to drive the highest volumes. What has changed is the absolute intensity: 2023 and 2024 are visibly darker than 2022, indicating a steady month-by-month improvement. The slope chart makes the divergence between markets explicit: India and the United States show steep upward slopes, while China's line points in the opposite direction, a gap that has profound implications for total industry value given how much Chinese visitors spend per trip.

Key insight

India has emerged as one of Australia's fastest-growing international tourism markets, supported by strong growth in visiting friends and relatives travel, education, and an expanding middle class with increasing outbound travel demand.

Finally, the map below shows how each Australian state and territory has fared in recovering its international visitor base, expressed as a percentage of 2019 arrival levels. This geographic view reveals whether recovery has been nationally uniform or whether some states have benefited more than others from the return of international travel.

States with diversified international visitor bases — particularly those less dependent on Chinese arrivals — have recovered more completely. NSW and Victoria, as the primary entry points for most international visitors, reflect the aggregate national recovery closely. States more heavily reliant on a single source market show greater variance, underscoring the risk that comes with geographic concentration of international visitor flows.

Key insight

China remains Australia's largest unrecovered tourism market by both visitor volume and spending. The speed of China's long-term recovery will play a major role in shaping the future growth trajectory of Australia's tourism industry, particularly for destinations heavily reliant on international leisure travel.

Australia's tourism industry has proven its resilience — but resilience is not the same as full recovery. The visitor economy that emerges from this period will look different from the one that existed before 2020. Which markets grow, which states benefit, and which sectors attract investment will depend on decisions made now about aviation capacity, destination marketing, and the long-term relationship with key overseas markets, particularly China. The numbers tell a story of cautious optimism: strong domestic demand, a recovering international base, and new growth markets emerging — but tempered by the recognition that the road back for some of Australia's most valuable tourism relationships is still unfinished.
